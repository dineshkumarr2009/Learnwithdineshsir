BLOCK 01 / 14 HRS

The Learning Engine

Before anyone can be motivated or managed, they have to learn. Every skill, habit and bad reflex on your team was learned, which means every one of them can be reshaped. This block hands you the controls.

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience. Three words carry the weight. Permanent, so a one-off fluke does not count. Behaviour, so it has to show up in what a person actually does, not just what they say they know. Experience, so it is earned through practice, observation or consequence, never installed by a memo. If you can change behaviour and make it stick, you have made someone learn, and that is the quiet core of all management.

📖 OB canon · Learning theories

Three theories of how learning happens

Your syllabus names three lenses. Each explains a different slice of how behaviour is acquired, and a complete manager uses all three.

  • Stimulus-Response (behaviourist): learning is a bond between a stimulus and a response. Classical conditioning (Pavlov) pairs a neutral cue with an automatic reaction until the cue alone triggers it, the involuntary root of workplace associations, like dread at a certain ringtone. Operant conditioning (Skinner) is stronger for management: behaviour is shaped by its consequences, so what gets rewarded gets repeated.
  • Cognitive theory (Tolman): learning is not just reflex. People build mental maps and expectancies, and can learn without immediate reward, then use the knowledge later when it pays. Thinking sits between the stimulus and the response.
  • Social learning (Bandura): we learn by watching others and copying models we find credible or admired, through attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. This is why a leader's visible behaviour teaches more than any policy document.

📖 Robbins & Judge · Ch 7

Shaping: building a big behaviour one step at a time

Shaping means reinforcing successive approximations, rewarding each small step that moves closer to the target behaviour instead of waiting for the finished article. It is how you teach anything complex, from a new CRM habit to a full sales pitch.

  • Start where they are. Reward the first rough attempt, not perfection.
  • Raise the bar in stages. Each round, reward only the closer version.
  • Reward often early, then thin out. Frequent reinforcement to build the habit, spaced reinforcement to make it durable.
  • Never punish the attempt. Punishing an imperfect step kills the willingness to try the next one.
Why it beats a training manualPeople do not leap to competence. Shaping turns an overwhelming skill into a ladder of small, winnable steps, and each rung earns a reward that pulls them up.
The operating principles

Principles of learning to design into any training

Whatever the method, learning sticks better when you build in a few conditions: motivation and readiness (the learner must want it and be prepared for it), active practice and repetition (behaviour changes through doing, not hearing), timely feedback and reinforcement (the sooner the consequence, the tighter the lesson), meaningful, whole-to-part material (context first, then detail), and transfer (practice in conditions close to the real job so the skill carries over). Ignore these and even good content fails to land.

Learning is not only about performance. Learning for green behaviour applies the same engine to sustainability, turning eco-friendly actions into habits through modelling, reinforcement and shaping. This is where organisational behaviour meets human values.

Model

Make it visible

Leaders and green champions publicly practising sustainable habits teach the team through social learning far faster than a policy poster ever will.

Reinforce

Reward the act

Recognise and reward energy saving, waste cutting and car-pooling. What gets reinforced gets repeated, so green wins must be celebrated, not assumed.

Shape

Start small

Begin with one easy habit, a reusable bottle or a lights-off rule, then raise the bar in stages until sustainability is simply how the office runs.

BLOCK 02 / 14 HRS

The Perception Filter

Your team does not respond to reality. They respond to their reading of it. Perception is the lens every fact passes through before it becomes a decision, and it distorts far more than anyone admits.

Perception is the process by which we organise and interpret sensory impressions in order to give meaning to our environment. The crucial, uncomfortable truth is that what we perceive can be substantially different from objective reality. Two people see the same meeting, the same email, the same performance, and walk away with opposite stories. Since people act on their perception, not on the facts, perception is reality as far as behaviour is concerned. Manage the perception, or it will manage you.

📖 Robbins & Judge · Ch 6

The three factors that bend perception

Robbins locates the distortion in three places. When a reading feels off, one of these three is usually the cause.

  • The perceiver: your own attitudes, motives, interests, past experience and expectations colour what you see. You do not see people as they are, you see them as you are.
  • The target: features of the person or thing being judged, novelty, motion, sound, size, proximity and similarity. Loud or unusual targets grab attention, and we wrongly group similar things together.
  • The situation: the context, timing, and work or social setting. The same person reads as confident in a boardroom and arrogant at a funeral. Context reframes everything.

Tap each factor to see how it quietly rewrites what your team sees before they have consciously judged anything.

The Perceiver You

A manager expecting a hire to fail will notice every stumble and miss every win. Your expectation becomes a filter, then a self-fulfilling prophecy. Audit your own assumptions before you audit the person.

The Target Them

The loudest voice reads as the most competent, the newest face draws the most scrutiny, and we assume people who look or sound alike think alike. Salience is not the same as substance.

The Situation Context

Time pressure, the room, who else is present, all reframe the same behaviour. Judge the performance, then check whether you are really judging the situation it happened in.

Now watch your own perception fail in real time. This is Daniel Simons' famous selective attention test. Follow the instructions before you read on. Most people, focused on one task, are completely blind to something impossible happening in plain sight. That blindness is exactly what you carry into every performance review.

BLOCK 03 / 14 HRS

Reading People

Every day you explain why people did what they did, and most of the time you are wrong in a predictable direction. This block hands you the engine professionals use to judge fairly, and the shortcuts that sabotage everyone else.

When someone behaves, we instantly build a story about why. Attribution theory says we decide whether a behaviour was internally caused, under the person's own control, or externally caused, forced by the situation. That single judgment drives whether you coach someone or fire them. And we make it using three signals, whether we know it or not.

📖 Robbins & Judge · Ch 6

The rules of attribution, and the two errors that break them

Kelley's model reads the three signals like this, and then two deep biases quietly corrupt the reading.

  • Distinctiveness: high (only this task) points external, low (everywhere) points internal.
  • Consensus: high (everyone did it) points external, low (only them) points internal.
  • Consistency: high (every time) points internal, low (a one-off) points external.
  • Fundamental attribution error: when judging others, we overweight their character and underweight their situation. The late employee is lazy, never mind the broken system.
  • Self-serving bias: for ourselves we flip it. Our wins are our talent, our failures are bad luck or someone else's fault.
The fair-manager habitBefore you attribute a failure to the person, force yourself to argue the situation's case first. It is the single fastest way to become fairer than your competitors.

Watch attribution and the fundamental attribution error explained cleanly before you move on. Notice how quickly we credit personality and forget the situation, the exact trap the Attribution Engine above is built to catch.

The Decision Architecture

A manager is paid to decide. This block shows you the ideal way decisions are supposed to work, the messy way they actually work, the biases that ambush them, and the ethical test every serious call must pass.

Every decision starts as a gap between how things are and how you want them to be. Organisations assume people then choose rationally. Reality is kinder to human limits. There are three modes running in every manager, and knowing which one you are in is half the discipline. Switch between them.

📖 Robbins & Judge · Ch 6

The rational model, and why nobody quite lives in it

The rational decision-making model assumes a clear problem, full information and a single goal, then runs six steps.

  • 1. Define the problem.
  • 2. Identify the decision criteria that matter.
  • 3. Weight those criteria.
  • 4. Generate the alternatives.
  • 5. Rate each alternative against the criteria.
  • 6. Choose the highest-scoring option.
Bounded rationality and intuitionHerbert Simon showed we rarely finish that process. We build simplified models, consider a few options and pick the first one that is good enough, called satisficing, not the perfect one. And under expertise and time pressure we lean on intuition, fast judgment distilled from experience. Neither is lazy, but both are where bias sneaks in.

Behavioural economist Dan Ariely shows, with visual illusions and real experiments, that our decisions are far less rational than we believe, and that whoever designs the default often makes the choice for us. This is bounded rationality made visible.

📖 Robbins & Judge · Ch 6

The three ethical decision criteria

Robbins names three frameworks people use to make ethical choices. Each protects something real, and each has a blind spot.

  • Utilitarianism: choose whatever delivers the greatest good for the greatest number. Efficient and results-focused, but it can quietly sacrifice a minority for the majority.
  • Rights: respect the fundamental rights and liberties of individuals, like privacy, free speech and due process. This is what protects whistle-blowers, but it can block decisions that would help the many.
  • Justice: impose and enforce rules fairly and impartially, so benefits and costs are distributed equitably. It protects the underrepresented, but can reward mediocrity by ignoring performance.
Cross-cutting noteReal ethical maturity is holding all three in tension, not picking the one that lets you do what you already wanted. Human values and professional ethics live in that discomfort.

BLOCK 05 / 14 HRS

Machine-Age Decisions

The newest member of every management team is an algorithm. This final block is about deciding with machines: what to hand them, what to keep, and how to stay ethical when the recommendation comes from a black box.

Robbins calls disciplined, evidence-first decision-making evidence-based management: base your calls on the best available data, not gut, tradition or the loudest voice. Data-driven decision-making and artificial intelligence are the modern engines of exactly that. Used well, they debias us. Used badly, they scale our worst biases to millions of decisions in seconds. The manager's new skill is not coding. It is knowing where a human must stay in the loop.

📖 Applied OB · AI & ethics

The manager's rules for deciding with machines

AI in managerial decision-making is powerful precisely where humans are weak, and dangerous precisely where humans matter most. Four rules keep it on the right side.

  • Augment, do not abdicate. The algorithm advises, the accountable human decides. Never hand a one-way-door, people-affecting call to a black box.
  • Interrogate the data. Biased training data produces biased decisions at scale. Ask what history the model learned from before you trust it.
  • Demand explainability. If no one can explain why the model chose, you cannot defend the decision, ethically or legally.
  • Keep the human in the loop for judgment, ethics and edge cases. Give AI the pattern-matching, keep the values, fairness and dignity calls for people.

The AI Hiring Tool Dilemma

👥 Groups of 5 to 6✋ One spokesperson⏰ 12 min📣 5 groups deliver a ruling

A vendor is selling you an AI that promises to cut hiring time by 70 percent. It was trained on your industry's past ten years of hires. Legal is nervous, the CFO loves the savings, and the decision is yours by end of week.

1

Interrogate the data. If the last decade of hiring was skewed, the model will learn and scale that skew. Ask what it was trained on.

2

Apply the three lenses. Utilitarian savings versus the rights of rejected candidates versus justice across gender and background.

3

Set the human-in-the-loop rule. Let it screen and rank, never auto-reject, and keep the final call with a human panel.

4

Demand explainability and audit. If the vendor cannot explain a rejection, you cannot defend it. Build the review before you buy.

Role-play then rule

Assign roles inside each group: CFO, Legal, HR and a rejected candidate. Argue it from all four seats, then deliver one ruling: yes, no, or yes with conditions, plus your non-negotiable safeguard.
